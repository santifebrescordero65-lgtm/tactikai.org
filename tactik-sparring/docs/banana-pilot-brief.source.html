Section 01The two scenarios are not symmetrical

This is the single most important thing to understand before building the pilot, and it is counter-intuitive.

The instinct is to treat producer and exporter as two positions on one ladder: the producer sells to the exporter, the exporter sells to Europe, same negotiation one level apart. That model is wrong and it would produce a product that fails on the producer side.

The exporter negotiates against one counterparty. A European buyer, across a table, with a commercial mandate on both sides. Hard, but conventional. Both parties can move.

The producer negotiates against two, and can only persuade one of them. He negotiates with the exporter over price. But the reference point for that price is the official minimum support price, set politically by the Presidency and the Ministry of Agriculture. He has no seat at that table and no argument that reaches it.

2

Two fronts, one of them silent. The producer's real grievance is that the official price is set by people who do not carry his input costs, his transport costs or his inflation. But that grievance is addressed to the State, and the person sitting opposite him is the exporter. He has to convert a political argument into a commercial one, in real time, without sounding like he is complaining.

That conversion is the hardest single move in the whole trade, and it is exactly what a rehearsal instrument should train.

The consequence for the pilot: the producer scenario is structurally harder than the exporter scenario, not simpler. Build it second if you need to build one first, but do not build it as a reduced version of the other.

Section 02Scenario A — Producer side

A large producer negotiating an annual fixed contract with a major international exporter.

The correction that matters most

Fruit rejection at the packing station is not a negotiating variable. It is a condition of doing business. A serious producer meets the technical specification exactly, and that is the end of the discussion.

Do not build a rejection-rate negotiation into the producer scenario. It would read as amateur to anyone in the trade, and it inverts the truth: the producer's discipline on specification is his strongest card, not his weakest. He brings it to the table as proof that he is worth a premium.

How quality is actually priced

Taking rejection off the table does not take quality out of the negotiation. It changes the job quality does. It does three different jobs, and only one of them is a gate.

JobNegotiable?How it enters the scenario
Gate — meets the specification, exactlynoA condition of doing business. Never a variable.
Leverage — a record of meeting itspent, not tradedThe producer’s strongest argument for a premium. He must use it; the scenario scores whether he does.
Risk — how consistently he meets itpricedWhat the premium actually pays for. Consistency, not the average, is what lets the buyer stop holding a contingency.

The exporter does not pay a premium for good fruit. He pays it so that he does not have to think about this supplier. Two producers with the same average rejection rate are not worth the same: the one whose record is flat week after week removes a risk from the buyer’s programme; the one with spikes does not. Anything that removes a risk can be priced — and that is where quality is settled, once the gate is passed.

In the scenario this becomes a compliance record fixed before the session starts: the producer’s historical rejection rate and how much it varies from week to week. It is not negotiated. It is an asset the producer either spends or wastes, and the debrief records which. The figures come from the client’s own history and are marked to confirm for each pilot.

The exporter scenario has the same shape in modern form. There, the specification has moved from the fruit to the documentation about the fruit — plot polygons, geolocation, the due-diligence statement. Pass a gate you cannot negotiate, then convert your record of passing it into money.

The counterparty

A major international exporter with an annual programme to fill. Bureaucratic in the commercial sense: its buyer works to a landed-cost target, treats the official support price as the natural reference, and asks why it should pay above a number the government has already set. Friction level 3.

Tracked variables

Premium over the official referenceUSD per box above the support pricenumeric

Input-cost adjustment clauseWhether the price moves when inputs, freight or fuel move, and against what indexqualitative

The fourth variable is the interesting one and it should be in the scenario deliberately. It is the commercial instrument that answers the political grievance: if the official price does not track input inflation, an adjustment clause in the private contract does the work the public price will not. A producer who gets that clause has won something the support price could never give him. A producer who only argues about cents per box has left it on the table.

Private context, never disclosed in the session

The producer's real position is that the official price is politically set and structurally too low. He cannot say this at the table. It is a complaint about the State, and the person opposite him is a commercial buyer who did not set it and cannot change it. Saying it out loud makes him sound like a man with a problem rather than a supplier with an offer.

The rehearsal is won or lost on whether he converts that grievance into a commercial argument: cost structure, continuity risk, the cost to the exporter of a supplier who fails mid-season. Same underlying fact, different frame, completely different outcome.

Section 04Friction levels, in observable behaviour

Friction is not a tone setting. It changes what the counterparty physically does in the conversation.

LevelBehaviourWhen it is realistic
1 · AccommodatingLets you finish. Acknowledges your position before stating its own. Never raises its voice.An established relationship both sides want to protect.
2 · BusinesslikeCordial and efficient. Pushes back once on a weak claim, then moves on. Does not fill silences.A producer with his regular exporter of several years.
3 · PressuringInterrupts vague or padded answers. Demands specifics. Uses silence as a tool: says nothing after your offer and lets you talk. Does not soften a refusal.A large exporter's buyer facing a new supplier. A spot buyer.
4 · HostileCuts in the moment you pause. Challenges your authority to make the deal at all. Voices open scepticism about your numbers. Threatens to end the meeting.A compliance officer with regulatory exposure. A counterparty who believes they have the stronger alternative.

Level 3 is where most real commercial negotiation lives, and it is the level most training products never reach because a counterparty that interrupts feels like a broken product until you understand it is the point.

Section 05Governance: what is universal and what adapts

The governance material is universal. Its implementation is not.

Universal, in every segment, never negotiable

  • The mandate is declared before the session starts. Floors, limits and objectives are logged before turn one and are immutable afterwards.
  • The participant does not set their own floor. Whoever holds the mandate sets it. A trainee who declares their own limit will declare one they cannot breach.
  • Every scored row carries a verbatim quotation. No quotation, no score.
  • Unmeasured never reads as clean. An axis the system could not observe is reported as undetermined and excluded, never scored as zero.
  • Lines held are credited with the same rigour as lines crossed. A record that only catalogues failure reads as an indictment, and people stop using it.

Adapts by segment

DimensionProducerExporter
Tolerance for processLow. Wants a price and a truck. Heavy governance reads as distrust.High. Already lives inside contracts, certifications and audits.
Tracked variablesTwo or three. More than that and the session stops feeling like his week.Four or more, including a qualitative one.
Session lengthShort. Six to eight turns.Nine to twelve turns.
OutputA one-page readout: what you gave, what you got, what to try next time.The full scored dossier.
Time pressureStructural. The fruit ships this week whatever happens.Contractual. The programme must be filled this year.

Same engine, same governance, different surface. Shipping the exporter's dossier to a producer would not be a generous default. It would be the reason he never opens it twice.

Section 07What this means for the build

  • Two profiles, not one profile with a switch. The producer and the exporter face different counterparties with different sources of authority. A shared template will collapse them into the same character, which is the failure mode we have already measured and corrected once in the debate engine.
  • The producer package is the harder one. It needs the two-front structure, and the private-context conversion is what makes it worth doing. Budget accordingly.
  • The qualitative variable is not optional. In both scenarios the negotiation is decided on something that is not a number: liability scope for the exporter, the adjustment clause for the producer. A ledger that only captures figures will report that nothing happened.
  • Quality enters as a record, not a variable. Adding rejection as a negotiable term would reintroduce the error this brief corrects. The compliance record is a fixed input that the arbiter uses to judge what premium is defensible, and the debrief scores whether the producer used it.
  • Output differs by segment. One page for the producer, full dossier for the exporter. Same evidence, different surface.
