## Post (English)

The model can argue perfectly and never make an offer.

We stress-tested our own platform. The AI I build with ran a full negotiation on it, end to end, the way a user would.

Nine turns against an AI counterparty.

It knew the regulation. It pushed back on every point. And in nine turns it never once named a price, a volume, a date or a term.

This is what it reported back:

"I noticed it as a feeling before I could name it: I was working, but I was not getting anywhere."

"It was producing fluent, domain-accurate text. It was simply not producing a negotiation."

Then came the part that matters more.

Success criteria were written down before the fix. The fix was deployed. The measurement said nothing had changed. The real cause was somewhere else entirely. The next measurement met every criterion set in advance.

"The most impressive thing I saw was not the platform. It was that loop."

What that means for us:

We did to ourselves exactly what TACTIK does for a client.
Declare the limits before the table.
Measure what actually happened.
Publish the gap. Do not reframe it.

The rule that came out of it: the model that speaks never holds the limits.

That separation is what makes the real question answerable: did you hold your limits — and what did each concession buy you?

Measured in a second engine, turns that put a real term on the table went from 0 of 6 to 5 of 6.

This was our own test, not independent validation. That gate is still ahead.

Forty years at negotiating tables taught me that an argument is not an offer. It holds for machines too.

Before your next real table: could anyone reconstruct why you conceded what you conceded?
